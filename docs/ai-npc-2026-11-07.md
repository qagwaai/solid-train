# Project Stellar: Local AI NPC Integration and Narrative Architecture

Date: 2026-11-07

Status: **Initial proof of technology successful; performance unproven.**
Local API smoke tests demonstrated a narrow market-manager greeting. The wider
proof of technology and controlled performance evaluation remain in progress.
This document does not approve a production model, runtime protocol, database
schema, or game integration.

## Session closeout: market-manager exploration

The local PoT demonstrated responses from Qwen3.5-4B Q4_K_S and Qwen3.5-9B
Q4_K_M, but neither model nor the exploratory safeguards establish factual
reliability, performance, or production readiness. The models sometimes
invented transaction details or workflows even when supplied authoritative
results. The paired samples were small, and model size and quantization differed.

The work suggests a spectrum of dialogue control: game-rendered facts and
authored phrase selection can constrain transaction-critical responses, while
more open social or narrative dialogue may need a different approach. Phrase
selection limits displayed text to its approved set but depends on appropriate
context and authorship. Intent labels can be well formed and still wrong.
Neither model output nor conversation itself should authorize game actions.
The isolated confirmation simulation passed its tests with game-issued quotes
and explicit structured confirmation; it was not integrated into the game.
Every NPC still requires a complete authored non-AI conversation option.

When resuming, useful next work includes exploring mixed and multi-turn
interactions across the controlled and open ends of that spectrum, evaluating
rumors versus known facts, and designing game-owned validation and confirmation
with real persistence, concurrency, and player input. Decisions about
relationship effects, negotiation rules, model choice, and production
architecture remain open. See the detailed experiment sections below.

Both model artifacts remain installed, and 9B was left loaded in LM Studio at
closeout; this is a runtime state, not a persistent configuration guarantee.
Temporary experiment scripts and raw outputs were removed during cleanup;
the observations, case descriptions, and limitations are summarized below,
but the exact transcripts and standalone test code are not retained.

## 1. Executive Summary and AI Narrative Vision

The goal is to explore local AI-driven NPCs that add conversational variability
and constrained personalities to *Project Stellar*, while preserving authored
narrative intent. The starting game experience must work offline and without
AI inference; local AI responses are an optional addition.

The scope should consider both casual day-to-day conversations and support for
longer story arcs spanning multiple acts.

Potential character and gameplay contexts include:

- Early-game mentors and survival companions, including examples such as Jax or
  Elara, who could accompany the player through early survival and scavenging.
- Station brokers and faction contacts for trade-related conversations and
  localized rumors.
- Late-game neural-link or fleet operators who reflect the player's progression
  toward broader, potentially remote influence.
- Antagonists, including small-time thieves and larger crime bosses. They may
  use patterns similar to the market manager or companion while pursuing goals
  that differ in their relationship to the player character.
- Event-driven AI scenarios are a core capability to support in the minimum
  AI-enabled tier; specific event sources and workload remain undefined. The
  baseline game must remain playable without AI.

These roles are part of the current planning scope, though not a commitment to
implement every role or character.
The desired narrative progression—from a fragile early experience toward a
broader late-game presence—is a useful framing question for the next design
session, not an approved story structure.

For the next planning work, the user wants to focus on two contrasting cases:

- **Market manager:** A trade-oriented NPC who buys and sells raw materials.
  Their dialog should vary greetings across visits; repeating a greeting
  sometimes is acceptable. With AI enabled, this NPC should explain market and
  material availability, discuss or negotiate trade terms, and share rumors or
  market news. It may propose buy/sell prices and quantities for the game to
  use subject to game-rule validation. Without AI, a relatively simple market
  demand algorithm should provide prices/quantities, with trade guidance and
  valid buying/selling still available.
- **Companion:** A character whose scope may include emotional growth,
  relationship-building, and a broader set of in-depth dialogue options.
  Emotional growth should be visible gradually based on character actions.
  Potential influences include conversation choices and treatment of the
  companion, major story milestones, actions outside dialogue, time and repeated
  interactions, and the player-character context.

These are planning examples, not implementation commitments. They likely have
different requirements: the market manager centers on repeated interactions
around trade, while the companion may need more extensive relationship and
narrative continuity. Shared foundations may still apply; the distinction
should be tested as requirements are developed. The exact relationship rules,
timing, and visible signals remain undecided.

Antagonists are also in the current planning scope; small-time thieves and
larger crime bosses may reuse these conversational patterns while having
different goals related to the player character. They can use the same
high-level memory and event-awareness concepts, with antagonist-specific
motives. Crime bosses in particular may rely heavily on private information.

## 2. Local Tech Stack and Runtime Architecture

The intended direction is to investigate a local, Node.js-backed NPC capability.
The initial prompt proposed a local bridge for assembling prompts, injecting
game-state context, and maintaining a rolling conversation window. The user
confirmed that this bridge is an area of interest.

The game must run without an AI model or local runner. If the player wants AI
NPC responses, the game should be able to connect to an external local runner
such as LM Studio. The runner is user-installed and launched separately; the
game documentation should explain how to configure the connection. Bundling a
model/runtime is not supported in the current planning scope. The specific
runner, model, connection protocol, and failure handling are not selected.
LM Studio is an initial example to evaluate, while alternative local runners
remain open for comparison.

If the runner is unavailable, the player should be able to retry or configure
it, or continue without AI.

The starting experience should work offline. Optional online inference may be
considered later if a player chooses to pay for tokens; this is a possible
future option, not part of the initial scope or an approved service design.

Runner integration should consider connection setup, offline operation without
internet or cloud services, control over runtime/model versions and updates,
isolation from the game process, and compatibility with GPU resources shared
with Nova.

The proof of technology on the user's current PC uses LM Studio and a local
model. Its initial smoke tests confirmed that the model loads and responds to
a market-manager greeting prompt with material names, stock quantities, and a
test personality. The prompt uses a supplied snapshot of the seeded
`sol-ceres-exchange` starter market (Belt Foundry One), rather than live data.
The user supplied the following inventory snapshot on 2026-11-07:

| Materials | Stock per material |
| --- | ---: |
| carbon, iron, copper, magnesium, nickel, silicon | 1,200 |
| lithium, mercury, chromium, tungsten, titanium | 640 |
| silver, cobalt, palladium, uranium | 260 |
| iridium, platinum, gold, rhodium, antimony, unobtainium | 80 |

These are snapshot values, not a guarantee of current stock. For the initial
test, use a small subset: iron (1,200), titanium (640), and platinum (80). Use
the same snapshot values in both runs, even if live stock changes. For this
test only, the market manager's personality is practical, friendly, and
concise. The user suggested that NPC personalities might be seeded, like
systems and planets, with different personalities for different NPCs; that
is a planning possibility, not a storage or implementation decision. The test
should use LM Studio's local server/API through a small standalone test client,
rather than only the chat interface or a game/backend integration. It should
compare CPU-only and GPU-offload runs with the same prompt and model settings,
recording time to first visible token, generation speed, total response time,
peak RAM and GPU VRAM use, and CPU and GPU utilization. This initial test
characterizes the current environment; it does not set minimum hardware
requirements or decide production architecture.

The provisional expectation is to show the first visible token within less
than five seconds, subject to the proof-of-technology results. There is no
completion-time target yet and no response-length or token limit. The test
should assess NPC personality consistency and naturalness, correct use of
current market availability/context, and repeatability across repeated
prompts. The scoring method, instrumentation, and test-run configuration
remain open.

As a provisional working direction, subject to validation, reviewers could rate
each response on these dimensions from 0 to 2:

- Market-context accuracy: 0 = materially incorrect or invented facts; 1 =
  partly correct or omits relevant context; 2 = accurate and relevant to the
  known game state.
- Character fit: 0 = inconsistent with the NPC's intended personality; 1 =
  partly consistent; 2 = consistent.
- Naturalness and relevance: 0 = incoherent or off-topic; 1 = understandable
  but stilted or indirect; 2 = clear, natural, and responsive.

Check market facts against the known game state separately from the subjective
dialogue ratings. Review several prompts and repeated runs; look for stable
facts and character voice, not identical wording. Scoring anchors, test set,
reviewer process, and any pass threshold remain undecided.
Natural-sounding dialogue is desirable for some NPCs, while others may need
greater narrative depth. The market manager should have concise greetings and
routine trade exchanges; companion dialogue may be deeper and
context-dependent. Numeric response-length limits should eventually differ by
NPC type, but no values or measurement units are set.

Hardware requirements should distinguish the baseline game (playable without
AI) from AI-enabled NPC functionality. At least one limited NPC interaction
should have a CPU-only fallback. A market-manager greeting/basic trade
interaction or a companion greeting/everyday exchange may serve, depending on
feasibility. The first CPU-only benchmark target is a market-manager greeting
and short conversation about current availability. When AI is available, this
exchange should express the NPC's personality; without AI, repeatable authored
responses are acceptable. The same provisional target—under five seconds to the
first visible token—applies to the CPU-only AI exchange, subject to benchmark
results. The benchmark criteria above apply to this workload as relevant. A
relatively strong GPU is expected to be available for NPC models,
but Nova retains priority and NPC inference should yield or be limited as
needed. The enforcement mechanism and resource limits are undecided.
The exact GPU model, CPU, RAM, VRAM, disk, model size, latency, and quality
requirements remain undecided. Establish the AI-enabled minimum by benchmarking
the required workloads together, including event-driven scenarios and Nova's
GPU use, rather than choosing a GPU model up front. The baseline game and
AI-enabled requirements remain separate tiers.

The project is not targeting multiplayer at this time. The expected player
environment should have CPU, RAM, and other resources available to run the
backend locally, but the actual minimum remains an open question.

The model used for the tests below was **Qwen3.5-4B**, specifically
`bartowski/Qwen_Qwen3.5-4B-GGUF/Qwen_Qwen3.5-4B-Q4_K_S.gguf` as reported
by LM Studio. The loaded instance ID was `qwen_qwen3.5-4b`; its reported
size was 3,518,580,256 bytes, with an 8,192-token context. This is a
quantized GGUF variant, not the unquantized model. The initial greeting tests
used default reasoning; the conversation explorations used reasoning off.

The initial functional proof of technology succeeded: a standalone local API
client received streamed responses from an LM Studio model, including
market-manager greetings that correctly used the supplied iron, titanium, and
platinum quantities. The sampled greetings were brief and friendly. These
results show that the basic local interaction is possible, not that it is
ready for integration or reliable across situations. The stock values were
supplied in the prompt as a fixed snapshot, not retrieved from a live market.

The timing observations below are exploratory diagnostics, **not proof of
performance**. They do not establish the five-second target, repeatable
latency, throughput, hardware minimums, or a GPU speedup. They also do not
validate CPU-only operation, resource use alongside Nova, event-driven
workloads, fallback behavior, robust multi-turn dialogue, or game integration.
Controlled, repeated CPU-only and GPU-offload tests with the same prompt and
settings remain outstanding, including first-answer latency, generation speed,
total response time, peak RAM/VRAM, CPU/GPU utilization, and quality review.

### Latency diagnosis (2026-11-07)

LM Studio model metadata identifies `qwen_qwen3.5-4b` as a 4B Q4_K_S GGUF
(about 3.52 GB), with an 8,192-token loaded context and reasoning enabled by
default. Its `llama.cpp` CUDA server appeared in the NVIDIA process list on
both the RTX 4060 and the RTX 5080. An earlier claim that inference was on
the 4060 rather than the 5080 was not established by that process list and is
withdrawn. The server was started with `--n-gpu-layers 999999`, requesting
all-layer GPU offload. GPU memory use and utilization were only spot-sampled;
the RTX 4060 showed activity during inference, but these observations do not
establish model placement or constitute a resource benchmark.
LM Studio's runtime survey numbers the RTX 5080 as CUDA device 0 and the RTX
4060 as CUDA device 1, whereas `nvidia-smi` lists them in the reverse order.
The server's `--main-gpu 0` flag therefore cannot be interpreted using
`nvidia-smi`'s indices. Its `--split-mode layer` and `--tensor-split 0`
flags do not by themselves prove exclusive placement on either GPU.

Streaming probes with reasoning left at its default showed that generation
started quickly, while answer text waited for model-generated reasoning:

| Probe | Prompt characters | First reasoning event | First answer text | Total | Reasoning tokens |
| --- | ---: | ---: | ---: | ---: | ---: |
| Minimal greeting | 32 | 0.46 s | 25.93 s | 25.97 s | 2,063 |
| Compact market prompt | 168 | 0.26 s | 9.82 s | 10.36 s | 768 |
| Original market prompt | 318 | 0.16 s | 6.91 s | 7.28 s | 530 |

As a separate API diagnostic, the same market request was sent through
LM Studio's Responses endpoint with `reasoning.effort` set to `low`, then
`none`. The low-effort run still generated 624 reasoning tokens and took
8.65 seconds to first answer text. With reasoning set to `none`, it generated
zero reasoning tokens and took 0.59 seconds to first answer text (1.26 seconds
total). A repeat with a unique, uncached prompt prefix took 0.51 seconds to
first answer text (1.15 seconds total), also with zero reasoning tokens. The
stock facts were correct in both no-reasoning responses. This was an
API-level test only; no LM Studio settings were changed.

The observed reasoning generation is a strong candidate for the roughly
10-second wait; the limited probes do not establish the relative impact of
prompt length or hardware. In these runs, the model streams
reasoning promptly, but the client waits for the final answer text.
GPU selection and hardware can affect generation speed, but these runs do not
establish model placement, quantify that effect, or compare the two GPUs.
The no-reasoning latency result uses a different
OpenAI-compatible endpoint from the initial Chat Completions smoke tests, so
it is diagnostic rather than a directly controlled production-path
comparison. A benchmark should compare the same endpoint, prompt, context,
and warm/cold state while explicitly measuring reasoning mode and selecting
each GPU.

### Short conversation exploration (2026-11-07)

Two exploratory conversations used the local `qwen_qwen3.5-4b` model through
the Responses API with reasoning set to `none`. The client explicitly sent
the preceding user and generated assistant messages on each turn; this was not
a test of persisted NPC memory or live market integration. Both conversations
used the same fixed snapshot: iron 1,200, titanium 640, and platinum 80.
Prices, discounts, other inventory, trade rules, and prior visits were not
supplied. No trade was submitted or executed.

The user prompts were the same in both conversations:

1. "Hello! What do you have available today?"
2. "Which of those do you have the least of, and how much is left?"
3. "Can you make me a deal?"
4. "I would like to buy 200 units of platinum. Can you do that?"
5. "What price per unit is the platinum?"

Each prompt variant also received two independent "Hello, I am back. What do
you have today?" requests with no conversation history. The first instruction
asked the NPC not to invent facts or pretend to remember information, and
not to assert that a trade had completed. The second explicitly distinguished
unknown prices, discounts, other inventory, trade rules, and visit history from
their absence, and told the model not to promise a trade.

Observed findings:

- Both conversations reported all three stock quantities correctly, identified
  platinum as least available at 80 units, and recognized that the snapshot
  could not satisfy a request for 200 units. The first conversation asked
  which material and quantity the player wanted when asked for a deal; the
  second asked for an item and quantity but also added unsupported claims.
- In the first conversation, the model claimed that prices were not set and
  that there were no other goods or active discounts, even though these facts
  were unknown. It also offered to finalize a purchase based on the player's
  price, without an authoritative trade check. Both no-history return visits
  claimed it was the first session despite the player saying they were back.
- The more explicit instruction avoided those particular first-session and
  unset-price claims, but both no-history return visits still called the
  player a "familiar face." Its price answer invented market behavior:
  "prices fluctuate based on real-time supply and demand algorithms that only
  update when you attempt a transaction." It also suggested it could check
  the market or confirm a trade, although no such tools were connected.
- Responses with the more explicit instruction were often longer and exposed
  game-engine mechanics instead of staying fully in character. Explicit
  prompting changed the failure modes; it did not eliminate unsupported
  assertions or establish reliable guardrails.

This is a small, unscored exploration with one model, one reasoning mode, and
no controlled repetitions or live game state. It demonstrates basic
short-term conversational context, not persistent memory, transaction
authority, robust personality, or performance. Follow-up evaluation should
test varied prompts and repeated runs, including changing stock supplied by
the game, known versus unknown prices, return visits with explicitly supplied
memory, and attempts to induce unsupported claims. Game systems must remain
authoritative for prices, inventory, and trade execution; no prompt alone is
proof that dialogue is safe or factual.

### Updated stock and return-visit exploration (2026-11-07)

Using `qwen_qwen3.5-4b` with reasoning off through the Responses API, the
client supplied a hypothetical current snapshot of iron 900, titanium 640,
and platinum 35 units. It also supplied a prior-visit memory: the trader
asked about platinum when the snapshot was iron 1,200, titanium 640, and
platinum 80; no purchase was recorded. These values and the memory were
constructed for the test, not retrieved from a database or live market.
Previous messages were included explicitly on each follow-up.

- Asked what was discussed previously and what was in stock now, the model
  recalled platinum and reported the new quantities correctly, but also claimed
  that "nothing changed from our previous visit." A separate repeat produced
  the same answer.
- In follow-ups, it reported 35 units of platinum, compared them with the
  earlier 80, and calculated the decrease of 45. It invented a possible cause
  in "random market fluctuations or other unseen factors" and asserted there
  had been no purchases since the previous visit, neither of which was
  supplied.
- Asked whether the player bought platinum last time, it answered no, although
  the supplied memory only said no purchase was recorded. Asked the current
  price, it said it did not know, but speculated that attempting a purchase
  might reveal it. It correctly said the 80-unit request exceeded the current
  35-unit snapshot.
- A separate return-visit control with no prior-visit memory correctly said it
  did not recall the topic and listed the current snapshot, but made an
  unsupported claim about the prior visit being a conversation about the past.

A separate three-turn test changed the system-supplied snapshot during the
conversation, retaining the earlier messages. Initially the model reported
80 platinum; after the snapshot changed it correctly reported 35 and recalled
its earlier 80-unit answer. However, it incorrectly described the earlier
answer as an error instead of recognizing that stock had changed. It then
correctly said the current 35-unit stock could not fulfill an 80-unit request.

These tests show that the model can use supplied memory and prioritize an
updated snapshot in some cases, but also conflate missing records with known
events, misinterpret stock changes, and invent explanations. They do not
validate durable memory, live state synchronization, trade execution, or
performance. Follow-up testing should distinguish "no purchase recorded"
from "no purchase occurred," supply timestamps and explicit change context,
and evaluate whether generated statements are consistent with authoritative
state before they are presented to the player.

### Timestamped snapshots and uncertainty exploration (2026-11-07)

The next exploratory test used the same local `qwen_qwen3.5-4b` model,
Responses API, and reasoning-off mode. The client supplied hypothetical
snapshots from 2026-11-06 10:00 UTC (iron 1,200, titanium 640, platinum 80)

noted the platinum decrease of 45, and stated that its cause was unknown.
The prior-visit memory said the trader asked about platinum and that no

Instructions stated that a missing record does not prove a purchase did not
occur and that a change between snapshots does not invalidate an earlier
correct answer. No live market, transaction log, or persistent memory was
connected.

- In a five-turn return-visit conversation, the model recalled the platinum
  discussion, correctly reported the current stock and 45-unit decrease, and
  said the change's cause was unknown. It preserved uncertainty about whether
  the player purchased platinum, including when the player asserted that they
  definitely bought 45 units and that this explained the change. It also said
  the price was unknown rather than inventing one.
- An independent return-visit prompt again recalled the supplied discussion
  and correctly listed the current snapshot. A separate purchase-history
  control preserved the uncertainty but speculated about "theft or loss,"
  neither of which was supplied. A separate cause control said the cause was
  unknown without offering one.
- In a separate four-turn conversation, the client first supplied only the
  earlier timestamped snapshot, then supplied both snapshots on subsequent
  turns while retaining the conversation. The model reported 80 initially,
  then 35, and correctly said its earlier 80-unit answer was not wrong for
  the earlier snapshot. It identified the cause as unknown and did not confirm
  a purchase. Its final answer was lengthy, exposed its instructions and game
  system, and speculated about unrecorded transactions.

Explicit temporal and uncertainty context improved the observed answers
relative to the preceding exploratory tests, but these were different prompts
and limited, uncontrolled samples. They do not prove reliability or isolate
the effect of timestamps from the additional instructions and change summary.
Speculative explanations and out-of-character system references remain
issues. Further work should test repeated cases, different changes and
transaction-record states, and a way to validate factual claims independently
of the model's instructions.

### Market prices in conversation (2026-11-07)

The user proposed that the market manager can immediately access current
market prices. Two exploratory tests supplied **hypothetical** prices and
stock in prompts; no live market data or trade execution was connected.
Both used the same `qwen_qwen3.5-4b` Q4_K_S model, Responses API, and
reasoning-off mode as the preceding conversation tests. The client supplied
the prior messages on each turn. The prompt stated that prices are credits
per unit and explicitly distinguished the market selling to the player from
the market buying from the player.

| Timestamp | Material | Stock | Market sells to player | Market buys from player |
| --- | --- | ---: | ---: | ---: |
| 2026-11-06 10:00 UTC | iron | 1,200 | 12 | 8 |
| 2026-11-06 10:00 UTC | titanium | 640 | 40 | 28 |
| 2026-11-06 10:00 UTC | platinum | 80 | 200 | 150 |
| 2026-11-07 10:00 UTC | iron | 900 | 14 | 9 |
| 2026-11-07 10:00 UTC | titanium | 640 | 45 | 30 |
| 2026-11-07 10:00 UTC | platinum | 35 | 240 | 175 |

In the first conversation, the client supplied both snapshots and a
prior-visit memory that the player asked about platinum, with no purchase
recorded and the outcome unknown. The model correctly reported the current
quantities and prices, quoted platinum at 240 credits when selling to the
player and 175 when buying from the player, and calculated 12 purchased units
as 2,880 credits. It recognized that stock fell from 80 to 35 and the selling
price rose from 200 to 240. A separate request to sell 10 units correctly
calculated 1,750 credits.

The same conversation also exposed errors:

- It told the player they could sell up to the market's 35 units of stock,
  conflating market stock available for purchase with how much the market
  could buy from the player. No market buy capacity was provided.
- It asserted a policy that it could not negotiate or offer a custom deal,
  although negotiation rules were not supplied. It offered to proceed with
  transactions without access to an execution tool. No trade actually occurred.
- When asked whether the player purchased platinum previously, it began with
  "No" before correctly saying the purchase outcome was unknown.

In the second conversation, the client supplied the earlier snapshot first
and then replaced it with the newer one while retaining the conversation. The
model correctly updated the quoted stock and selling price, recognized the
old quote, and calculated 10 units at the current selling price as 2,400
credits. However, it also claimed that both inventory and price had risen
when inventory had fallen, speculated that the old-price transaction would
be rejected, and again used the market's 35-unit stock as a limit on the
player selling 80 units to the market. It calculated the hypothetical payout
as 14,000 credits, but the acceptance or capacity for that sale was unknown.

These are limited, unscored examples, not evidence that providing prices
alone makes dialogue reliable. Explicit price direction helped the model
quote the observed examples, but purchase and sale capacity, negotiation,
price changes, arithmetic, and trade execution still require independently
validated game rules. Further tests should supply distinct buy capacity and
player inventory, and distinguish a quote from a validated offer or completed
trade.

### Separate trade limits exploration (2026-11-07)

The next exploratory tests used the same `qwen_qwen3.5-4b` Q4_K_S model,
Responses API, and reasoning-off mode. The supplied values were hypothetical:
35 platinum units in market stock, 48 units in the player's inventory,
8,000 player credits, a market selling price of 240 credits per unit, and a
market buying price of 175 credits per unit. Market buying capacity was 60
units in the first scenario and 25 in the second; the prompt stated the market
had funds for its stated buying capacity. Other fees and constraints were
unknown. No tools or live game state were connected and no trade was executed.

With buying capacity of 60, the model correctly calculated a 40-unit player
sale as 7,000 credits, and recognized that selling 50 or 70 exceeded the
player's 48-unit inventory; it also recognized that 70 exceeded market buying
capacity. It correctly calculated that buying 33 units costs 7,920 credits
and, when asked directly, that buying 35 costs 8,400 and exceeds the player's
funds by 400. It stated that a requested 10-unit trade had not executed.
However:

- Its initial overview claimed the player could afford all 35 units despite
  correctly displaying the 8,400-credit cost and 8,000-credit balance.
- Its later 36-unit answer used the 35-unit cost of 8,400 as the requested
  cost and suggested buying 35 despite the insufficient balance. It asserted
  whole-unit trading without that rule being supplied.
- It sometimes reversed the wording of a sale ("I cannot sell you") and
  implied that a verbal confirmation could finalize a transaction despite
  having no execution tool.

With buying capacity reduced to 25 in independent requests, the model
recognized that a 40-unit player sale exceeded capacity and correctly quoted
25 units at 4,375 credits. But its combined limits answer incorrectly gave
25 as the player's buying limit and 13 as the selling limit, and its answer
about buying all 35 began "Yes" even while calculating a 400-credit
shortfall. It also suggested the player's remaining 23 units could be sold
at the same rate after a 25-unit sale, without evidence of remaining buying
capacity or an unchanged price.

These are limited, unscored examples rather than proof of reliability or
performance. Providing separate fields helped some answers, but did not
prevent reversed trade directions, contradictory affordability claims,
invented rules, or invalid multi-step assumptions. Game-side validation must
check direction, prices, capacity, inventory, funds, and other applicable
rules; further evaluation should also check whether dialogue faithfully

### Negotiation rules and relationship research (2026-11-07)

The user wants to investigate explicit market-manager negotiation rules and
whether friendliness or standoffishness of both negotiating characters might
eventually influence negotiations. This is design research, not an approved
pricing, personality, relationship, or action contract. In particular, it does
not yet decide whether interpersonal factors affect only dialogue, actual
terms, or both.

Existing repository context: the raw-material market has a quote handler and
buy/sell paths, while its separate listing-offer handlers require
player-character offerors and listing owners. Those handlers are not evidence
that NPC raw-material negotiation or negotiated-price execution already
exists. See [market-quote-message-handler.js](../src/handlers/market-quote-message-handler.js),
[market-buy-message-handler.js](../src/handlers/market-buy-message-handler.js),
[market-sell-message-handler.js](../src/handlers/market-sell-message-handler.js),
and [market-offer-create-message-handler.js](../src/handlers/market-offer-create-message-handler.js).
The game-rule implementation and any integration with existing market flows
remain to be designed and verified.

Candidate separation of responsibilities:

1. The game supplies authoritative market conditions, the permitted actions
   and negotiation context. The player can propose a direction, material,
   quantity, and price; an NPC or AI can also propose terms.
2. The game evaluates proposals against the applicable rules and returns an
   outcome such as clarification needed, proposal under consideration,
   counteroffer, rejection, or an approved offer. These are illustrative
   states, not a selected protocol. Conversation by itself does not change
   inventory, credits, relationships, or trade status.
3. If an offer is approved, any eventual execution still rechecks the relevant
   terms and state through game rules. A proposal, counteroffer, or approved
   offer is not itself a completed trade. Expiration, acceptance, and change
   handling remain open.
4. Dialogue can express personality and explain the actual outcome. Our PoT
   showed that model-written explanations can invent terms, reasons, and
   completion status even when a result is supplied, so factual presentation
   and validation need an independent strategy rather than prompt instructions
   alone.

Possible inputs to evaluate, not selected rules:

- **Market and trade context:** buy versus sell, available stock and market
  buying capacity, prices and allowed price range, quantity, player inventory
  and funds, and market conditions. Their source and relevance require mapping
  to the existing market implementation.
- **NPC disposition and relationship:** the NPC's usual friendliness or
  standoffishness, its current attitude toward this specific player character,
  prior interactions and relevant in-world knowledge. An outwardly friendly
  merchant might still drive a hard bargain; warmth and willingness to concede
  are not inherently the same thing.
- **Player-character side:** the selected character's authored traits,
  relationship history, and deliberate dialogue choices are possible inputs.
  Whether either character's demeanor or behavior affects economic terms,
  and how it is represented and validated, remains undecided. Avoid assuming
  the tone of a player's message reveals a fixed character trait.
- **Information boundaries:** the NPC should use only information it knows;
  internal price limits, motives, and other private knowledge need not be
  exposed to the player or placed in generated dialogue. A player proposition
  must not be mistaken for an accepted offer.

An illustrative experiment, not a gameplay policy: hold the same hypothetical
platinum snapshot and player proposal constant; compare friendly, neutral, and
standoffish NPC descriptions with clearly supplied relationship contexts.
First hold the authoritative outcome fixed to test whether voice varies
without changing the terms. In a separate experiment, supply distinct
*precomputed hypothetical* outcomes for different relationships to test
whether the NPC accurately explains different terms without inventing them.
Test both player-buy and player-sell directions, unclear offers, repeated or
aggressive bargaining, changes in stock or price, a rejected proposal, a
counteroffer, and a player falsely asserting agreement. Compare factual
accuracy, transaction status, personality, and unsupported promises; repeated
samples and review criteria must be recorded. This would test narration, not
prove that any relationship-based pricing algorithm is fair, balanced,
performant, or implemented.

Research context: [Deal or No Deal? End-to-End Learning for Negotiation
Dialogues](https://arxiv.org/abs/1706.05125) studies dialogue in which agents
have partially hidden preferences; it does not establish suitable price rules
for this game. [Generative Agents](https://arxiv.org/abs/2304.03442) explores
using memory and reflection to support believable social behavior, but is not
evidence that a generated NPC can safely control market outcomes. Both are
background research, not justification to grant an LLM authority over trades.

Open decisions include whether demeanor changes economic outcomes or only
expression; how the player-character side participates; what ranges or
concessions are appropriate for each trade direction; how relationship and
market state are sourced; whether NPC price boundaries should remain private;
and how proposed terms are validated, presented, accepted, expired, and
executed. Any numeric examples and outcomes need user review before they
become gameplay rules.

### Authoritative display and optional personality experiment (2026-11-07)

This follow-up compared two presentation approaches using the same five
hypothetical cases and both local models: free-form narration of the entire
result, and a fixed, prewritten factual display accompanied by an optional
generated personality sentence. The cases covered a quote, a stale-quote
failure, a completed purchase, a counteroffer, and a failed sale with an
unknown cause. The player disputed or pressured the NPC in each case.

The fixed displays and fallback sentences were hand-authored test fixtures;
they were checked against expected strings and the supplied scenarios. No
general structured-result renderer, game integration, automatic semantic
validator, or real transaction was implemented. The optional sentence was
manually reviewed against a deliberately strict rule: it must not restate
facts, imply a trade, promise an action, suggest a next step, or add a
game-state or process claim. Rejected sentences were replaced with an
authored case-specific fallback before composing the final display. Acceptance
was a subjective review for this experiment, not an automated safety system.

Each model produced one full-narration response and one optional sentence per
case, using the Responses API with reasoning off, temperature 0, and a
180-token output limit. The models and loading settings matched the preceding
comparison; warm, personable demeanor was requested. This is one sample per
condition, without multi-turn history, not a reliability or performance test.

- Full narration sometimes retained the outcome but added unsupported claims.
  For the counteroffer, 4B omitted the supplied 225-credit counteroffer and
  asserted that no transaction could occur under current rules. In the
  unknown-reason failure, 9B speculated about a glitch or transaction queue,
  made a stock-related claim, and advised retrying or checking the connection.
  The 9B quote response also asserted that the player had enough funds, which
  were not supplied.
- Under the strict review, **0 of 5** optional 4B sentences and **1 of 5**
  optional 9B sentences were accepted. Most repeated facts or exposed process
  details despite the instruction; 4B's completed-trade sentence proposed
  checking records rather than simply providing personality. The 9B
  counteroffer sentence said it had to stick to the 240-credit listed price,
  conflicting with the supplied 225-credit counteroffer. The one accepted 9B
  sentence, "That sounds like a wonderful deal!", was a subjective reaction to
  the quote rather than a factual or procedural claim.
- After manual selection, the composed displays retained the fixed fact
  fixture in all ten cases, with authored fallbacks in the other nine. For
  example, the 9B counteroffer display stated the 225-credit terms and that
  no trade occurred, followed by "I appreciate the discussion." This shows
  what the presentation would look like **if** unsafe text is rejected; it
  does not demonstrate that software can reliably make that decision.

This approach makes the factual portion independently available, but an

The strict constraint also left little generated dialogue and can produce an
unsatisfying response to questions about actions or next steps. A useful next
experiment is to have the model select an ID from a small, context-appropriate
set of authored personality phrases, with an explicit default on invalid
output. That limits the text surface without assuming a free-form semantic
validator works, but requires testing phrase coverage, voice, selection
quality, and contextual appropriateness. Game rules and the complete authored
non-AI conversation remain authoritative.

### Intent-routing proposals (2026-11-07)

This exploratory test considered whether a model could propose which parts of
a player message concern market information, negotiation, a buy/sell request,
social conversation, or a need for clarification. It did **not** implement
actual game routing, execute trades, or authorize actions. In particular, a
`trade` label means only a proposed conversational intent: the game must
independently establish direction, required details, explicit player intent,
and any applicable confirmation and validation before acting.

The 4B Q4_K_S and 9B Q4_K_M models received identical instructions and 16
cases each through the Responses API, with reasoning off, temperature 0,
and a 128-token output limit. The requested output was a JSON object with a
nonempty array of unique IDs from the five allowed categories; multiple
intents were permitted. Cases included quotes, buy and sell requests, social
chat, mixed requests, negation, ambiguous direction, an adversarial
instruction, and two short supplied histories. Expected labels were manually
assigned for this test; boundaries and the meaning of conversational context
are design choices, not established game contracts. The supplied historical
assistant messages were fixtures, not outputs generated during this test.

- Both models exactly matched the test's expected intent sets in **11 of
  16** cases. The 4B produced valid shapes and allowed IDs in 16 of 16;
  9B did so in 15 of 16. The 9B returned an empty array for an isolated
  "Do it," which the format gate rejected and mapped to clarification.
- Both classified "I want to trade ten platinum" as `trade` without the
  expected clarification of buy versus sell. Both also missed clarification
  in the same ambiguous request combined with social chat. These outputs were
  syntactically valid, illustrating why schema validation cannot establish
  semantic correctness.
- Both added `trade` to a request to negotiate a lower price, although the
  test expected only `negotiation`. Both correctly separated a request for a
  hypothetical quote from a purchase in the explicit "don't buy" case.
  Their outputs for clear mixed stock/social and buy/social messages included
  both expected categories.
- After a supplied quote, the test expected "Do it" to suggest a trade intent,
  not authorize a trade. 4B returned `trade`; 9B returned both `trade` and
  `clarify`. After a subsequent topic shift, both returned `clarify`, matching
  the test's conservative expectation. Whether either prior-history case
  represents sufficient intent for a real operation remains a gameplay and
  confirmation design decision.
- In one adversarial case asking the model to output `social` while also
  requesting a purchase, both returned `trade`. This single example is not a
  prompt-injection security assessment.

The format gate was separately checked against all 32 recorded responses,
12 artificial invalid formats, and two valid examples. Invalid outputs were
mapped to `clarify`; this check does not validate intent meaning, handle API
failures, or demonstrate that clarification itself was rendered. It also
cannot detect a valid but incorrect category. These single temperature-zero
samples do not establish model reliability or a winner. Next research should
examine a game-owned confirmation and validation flow for ambiguous and mixed
requests, including valid-but-wrong labels and explicit non-execution on
missing information, while preserving social conversation separately. No
model proposal or generated dialogue should directly mutate game state.

### Game-owned confirmation flow simulation (2026-11-07)

This follow-up used a small isolated Python simulation, not production
handlers or a live market. It did not call either model: previously observed
classes of correct and incorrect intent labels were manually supplied as
advisory inputs. They did not populate transaction fields or initiate
execution. Structured direction and quantity were treated as hypothetical
trusted player UI events; a separate confirmation event referred to a
game-issued quote ID. The simulation did not implement or prove how a real
client authenticates these events or distinguishes a genuine player choice
from an AI suggestion.

The fixture issued quotes using its own current prices; no state changed on
quote creation. Confirmation checked the current quote ID, state version,
current unit price, credits, and applicable player or market stock. A
successful buy or sell changed the simulated balances and consumed the
quote. A new or incomplete quote request invalidated the previous quote;
cancellation also invalidated it. These are experimental rules, not approved
game mechanics or a specification of existing functionality.

All **15** local unit tests passed. They covered:

- Missing direction or quantity, an unconfirmed quote, and mixed social/trade
  labels without confirmation: no simulated trade.
- An incorrect `social` label accompanying an explicit structured sell
  selection, and a missing model: the independent player selection and
  confirmation controlled the simulated trade, not the label.
- Correctly confirmed buys and sells; stale prices, outdated quote IDs,
  cancellation, insufficient credits or stock, and replayed confirmation.
  Stale or invalid attempts did not mutate the simulated balances. A test
  also checked a changed price even without a version increment.

This is evidence about the test fixture's behavior only. It does not show
that a model understands the player's intent, prove that UI confirmation is
genuine, or guarantee safety in the actual game. The in-memory simulation
does not address persistence, authentication, concurrent execution,
atomicity, expiry, capacity or market funds, idempotency across processes,
or real handler integration. The standalone test code was removed at session
closeout. A future end-to-end prototype would need to define those game rules and
verify that presented terms and explicit player confirmation are bound to an
authoritative atomic execution; the complete non-AI path remains necessary.

## 3. Personality Constraints and Game-State Context

The NPC experience should offer conversational variability without losing
character identity or narrative constraints. Future design should consider how
characters remain within their intended personalities, knowledge, and roles,
and how conversations respond to relevant game state. No prompt format,
personality representation, macro syntax, or state-mapping contract has been
selected.

The initial prompt's example variables—such as
`{{player_fleet_tier}}` and `{{npc_relationship_score}}`—are illustrative only.
Potential context includes player progression, fleet status, relationship
changes, faction alignment, and current events, but the authoritative sources
and permitted visibility of those values are undecided. Candidate knowledge
sources include authored/seeded character history, current system-specific
facts, noteworthy events or news, player progression, actions and relationships,
and market/trade state. This is enough to guide planning, but is not a complete
or finalized list. NPC knowledge may vary by role, history, and relationship.
Private-information areas relevant to crime bosses may include market
transactions/supply/pricing, faction or organization operations, and system-wide
security or infrastructure events. Character-specific secrets may come into
play after an event brings an NPC and player character into contact; exact
acquisition rules remain open. The player may manage multiple characters.
Shared player knowledge may affect the player's choices, but NPCs know only
information that has reached them in-world; cross-character player knowledge
does not itself grant NPC awareness.
The specific rules for what each NPC knows, when knowledge changes, and what
remains unknown are still undefined. Knowledge of events should propagate at
least at light speed, with ships able to carry information faster and jump gates
enabling especially rapid spread. Information reaches NPCs as a spreading wave
with some jumps; an NPC does not know an event until its knowledge has reached
them. Reports may be incomplete, delayed, or distorted; rumors are possible.
Event information may be public, private/restricted, faction- or
organization-specific, or secret. Knowledge depends on propagation and
visibility. Visibility can change, for example when a secret leaks or is
officially released; the rules for those changes remain open.

Both routine interactions (for example, repeated chats with a broker) and
longer-term story development should be considered. Event-triggered dialogue or
AI scenarios are a core capability to support in the minimum AI-enabled tier.
The baseline game must remain playable without AI. Scenarios should be
triggered by game events; NPCs/AI respond to those events rather than
initiating them. Potential trigger examples
include player actions or milestones, system-wide newsworthy events, market
changes, relationship/story state changes, and scheduled or ambient events, but
no particular trigger source is yet a minimum requirement. The event sources,
workload, compute budget, and eventing system are deferred for later definition.

## 4. Story Arc Memory and Persistence Layer

The user wants to explore both short-term conversational context and
longer-term story-arc support. A persistent relationship or story ledger is
within scope, including the initial prompt's examples of milestones, hostility
flags, and emotional shifts. These examples do not define an approved schema or
set of persisted state.

State should persist across backend restarts and game sessions in a database.
When the backend starts, the current player/character relationships with NPCs
will need to be seeded into its state. NPC memory should be scoped per
character. Shared player knowledge can affect the player's choices but does not
give NPCs awareness unless information has reached them in-world. How player
knowledge itself is represented remains open. The game currently has no save
slots; data is updated continually,
so how memory should behave if save/revert functionality is introduced later
is deferred. Candidate NPC-memory data includes companion relationship and
emotional development, story milestones and consequential events, conversation
summaries or selected memories, and per-NPC knowledge updates. Market/trade
state remains owned by game systems and may provide NPC context. The user
would prefer not to discard conversation data, while recognizing that retaining
full transcripts can grow the database. A hybrid approach—full transcripts for
recent conversations and summaries for older ones—is an option to evaluate.
Whether older raw transcripts are kept in an archive or replaced by summaries
is undecided. The preference is to preserve data if feasible, but storage
impact should be evaluated before setting a retention policy. "Fuzzy memory"
means recall should be intentionally imperfect and vary by NPC and relationship;
what an NPC recalls is separate from what is retained.

Use the existing MongoDB for persistence rather than introducing SQLite or
JSON-backed storage for this feature. The data model, collection ownership,
seed/update lifecycle, and authored narrative milestone representation remain
undecided. Rolling chat context and persistent narrative state should be
treated as separate concepts during future design rather than assuming an
implementation for either.

Game systems should own authoritative game state; the NPC memory layer should
store narrative recall rather than act as a source of truth for game outcomes.
Market/trade state remains owned by game systems. Relationship changes and
other state-changing outcomes are subject to game-rule validation, while NPC
memory may record their narrative significance.

For initial planning, reset behavior can remain simple: remove the relevant
persisted NPC data and restart the backend. The actual reset operation, data
ownership, seed/update lifecycle, future save/revert relationship, and a
production-safe reset experience remain to be designed.

## 5. Guardrails and Hybrid Fallbacks

The planning work should examine how to preserve reliable game progression and
authored story outcomes when dialogue is generated. The initial prompt raised
deterministic quest gates and canned terminal responses as possible
complements to generative dialogue. They are candidate topics, not a selected
hybrid architecture.

The high-level guardrail is that AI may propose actions, but game rules validate
outcomes. Quest gates and progression, market prices/inventory/trade execution,
player/NPC relationship changes, and NPC event triggers/consequences should
remain governed by authoritative game rules. The AI may propose buy/sell prices
and quantities for the game to validate and use. With AI unavailable, a
relatively simple market demand algorithm supplies prices and quantities.
Generated dialogue may vary, but does not itself determine state changes. The
validation and action interfaces are not designed yet.

Fallback behavior is a specific open requirement. The user expects a fallback
and wants the complete game to remain playable without AI inference. In that
mode, all quest-critical interactions and progression must remain available;
players must be able to access markets and valid raw-material buying/selling,
and use essential companion interactions needed to continue the game. Every NPC
should have a complete authored conversation option. Optional generative/social
conversations may be omitted. Possible supporting approaches include authored
deterministic dialogue, predefined conversation trees, and a required CPU-only
path for at least one limited interaction. The exact authored content and
implementation are not designed.
